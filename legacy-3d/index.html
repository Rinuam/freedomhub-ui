05 — USE THIS TODAY

Six things that change
how you're seen as an engineer.

Nothing to sign up for. These are the habits that separate people who get promoted from people who get overlooked — in interviews and on the floor.

01

Answer with mechanism, not memory

“Pressure affects step coverage” is a fact. “Higher pressure shortens the mean free path, so arriving atoms scatter more and lose directionality — which improves sidewall coverage but hurts fill” is understanding. Interviewers hear the difference immediately.

02

Never say “it stopped working”

Say what changed, when, and what stayed constant. Every strong diagnosis starts by separating the variables that moved from the ones that didn't. This one habit makes you sound five years more experienced.

03

Learn to defend a number

If you report 42.3 nm, be ready for “how do you know?” Know your measurement uncertainty, your sampling plan, and whether the tool was calibrated. Engineers who can't defend their data don't get trusted with decisions.

04

One variable at a time is a beginner's rule

It's how you start, not how you finish. Designing a small factorial experiment — three or four factors at once — turns a month of guessing into a week of answers, and it's the most visible upgrade in how you work.

05

Write the failure down

Keep a private log of every problem you solved: symptom, hypothesis, test, result. Two years of that log is your interview material, your promotion case, and eventually the reason others come to you first.

06

Translate up, not down

Your manager doesn't need the chemistry. They need cost, risk and time. “Cpk went from 1.02 to 1.41, so we can drop the extra inspection step” is a sentence that gets budgets approved and names remembered.

The five-step troubleshooting loop

USE IT IN INTERVIEWS. USE IT AT THE TOOL.

  1. 01OBSERVEwhat exactly changed
  2. 02SPLITtool, process or metrology
  3. 03HYPOTHESISEa mechanism, not a guess
  4. 04DISCRIMINATEone test, two outcomes
  5. 05VERIFY & LOCKprove it, then document

Step 04 is where most people fail. A good test is one whose result rules something out whichever way it goes. If both outcomes leave you equally confused, it isn't an experiment — it's a hope.
